For many network engineers, the career path feels linear:

Help desk → NOC → operations → architecture → burnout.

You spend years surviving outages, maintenance windows, overnight migrations, and the constant pressure of touching production systems that thousands, sometimes millions, of people depend on.

But what if there's another path?

In a recent episode of The Art of Network Engineering, Andy Lapteff sat down with Patrick McCabe to discuss a career transition many engineers never seriously consider: moving from network engineering into vendor-side product marketing.

And surprisingly, it might be one of the best-kept secrets in enterprise IT careers.

## The Reality of Operational Networking

One of the most relatable parts of the conversation was the discussion around maintenance windows and outages.

Patrick shared stories from his early engineering days supporting massive carrier networks, including a catastrophic outage involving ATM switches, debug sessions, and an accidentally powered-off core network during a maintenance window.

If you've worked in operations long enough, you understand the emotional weight behind those stories.

Every maintenance window carries risk.
Every change feels like gambling with production.
Every outage leaves a mark.

As Andy joked during the episode:

> "You press that button, and you don't know what's going to happen."

That stress compounds over years.

For many engineers, the issue isn't that they stop loving technology.
It's that operational pressure eventually becomes exhausting.

## The Vendor Career Path Most Engineers Never Hear About

A major theme of the episode was awareness.

Most engineers know about:

* Network operations
* Architecture
* Consulting
* Cloud engineering

But far fewer understand vendor-side roles like:

* Systems Engineering (SE)
* Technical Marketing Engineering (TME)
* Product Marketing
* Developer Relations
* Product Management

These careers often allow engineers to:

* Stay technical
* Influence products
* Teach others
* Create content
* Help customers strategically
* Avoid constant operational firefighting

For Patrick, the transition started after years of technical support and systems engineering work.

For Andy, product marketing became the first role truly aligned with his natural strengths:

* Communication
* Storytelling
* Technical translation
* Relationship building
* Content creation

## Product Marketing Is Not "Just Marketing"

One of the most important takeaways from the discussion is that product marketing is its own professional skill set.

Engineers often assume:
"I know the technology, so I can explain it."

But product marketing requires a different mindset.

As Patrick explained, engineers naturally default toward detail:

* Deep technical implementation
* Configuration specifics
* Design minutiae

Marketing requires abstraction.

The challenge becomes:
How do you translate complex technical concepts into messaging that:

* resonates with customers,
* supports sales teams,
* differentiates products,
* and addresses real business pain?

Patrick told a story about writing his first "white paper" after transitioning into marketing.

The result?

A 100-page technical design guide.

Technically accurate?
Absolutely.

Useful marketing collateral?
Not exactly.

That moment captures the core shift engineers must make:
You are no longer only explaining how technology works.
You're explaining why it matters.

## Soft Skills Become Career Multipliers

Another recurring theme throughout the episode was communication.

Patrick repeatedly emphasized that his technical ability alone did not create career opportunities.

What helped him transition into customer-facing and marketing roles were skills like:

* communication,
* humility,
* relationship-building,
* listening,
* and the ability to connect with people.

This mirrors something many senior engineers eventually discover:

Technical skill gets you into the room.
Communication determines how far you go.

For engineers interested in expanding beyond purely operational work, communication skills become one of the highest-leverage investments possible.

That could mean:

* writing blogs,
* creating videos,
* public speaking,
* mentoring,
* or simply learning how to explain technical ideas clearly.

## Why AI Makes These Skills Even More Valuable

The episode also touched on AI and automation.

Importantly, neither Andy nor Patrick framed AI as replacing engineers outright.

Instead, the conversation focused on leverage.

Engineers who learn to use AI tools effectively will likely outperform those who ignore them.

That applies equally to:

* network automation,
* troubleshooting,
* documentation,
* content creation,
* and product marketing.

As Andy noted:
The people replaced by AI may not be engineers.
They may simply be engineers who refuse to use AI.

## The Bigger Career Question

At its core, this episode wasn't really about marketing.

It was about alignment.

Many engineers eventually realize they enjoy:

* teaching,
* communicating,
* influencing,
* mentoring,
* or building strategy

…more than they enjoy midnight outages and endless operational pressure.

Vendor-side careers can provide a path that still leverages technical experience while opening entirely different kinds of opportunities.

Not everyone will want that path.
And that's okay.

But more engineers should at least know it exists.
