I started writing this after run number 446.

I had just finished 5.5 miles in San Diego. It wasn't a race. I didn't set a PR. Nobody was waiting at the finish line. It was just a run. Afterward I opened Nike Run Club and looked at my stats.

1,508 miles.

More than 259 hours.

And 446 runs.

For some reason, the number that stuck with me wasn't the mileage. It was 446. Because 446 wasn't really a running statistic. It was 446 decisions. 446 times I had to put on my shoes and leave the house.

I absolutely did not want to make all 446 of those decisions. Some happened early in the morning when staying in bed would have been much easier. Some happened in brutal summer heat. Some happened during northeast winters when I was literally running around patches of ice. Some happened when my legs hurt, work was busy, I was tired, or I just didn't feel like doing it.

Some runs were great. Some sucked. Most were completely ordinary. And somehow all of those ordinary decisions turned into more than 1,500 miles.

That probably wouldn't mean as much to me if running had always been part of my life. It wasn't.

## I wasn't a runner

I stopped running decades ago. I had accumulated enough problems with my back and knee that eventually I accepted a pretty simple conclusion: I couldn't run anymore. Not "I'm taking some time off." Not "Maybe I'll get back to it." I just stopped thinking of running as something available to me.

I wasn't a runner.

Then I got laid off in December 2023. I was depressed. I was scared about what came next. I wasn't handling it particularly well. And I remembered something I hadn't thought seriously about in years: running used to make my brain work better. It changed my emotional state. It cleared my head. Problems that felt enormous before a run often felt more manageable afterward.

At that point I didn't care about pace or mileage. I wasn't trying to become an athlete. I wanted relief.

So I ran. Badly. Then I ran again.

In 2023, I logged 69 miles. In 2024, my first full year back, I ran 364.3 miles at an average pace of 13:11 per mile. In 2025, I ran 626.7 miles, and my average pace was 9:13. Almost four minutes per mile faster.

There wasn't one workout responsible for that difference. There was no dramatic breakthrough. No Rocky montage. Just a lot of runs.

## Humans don't improve like bank accounts

That's what got me thinking about one of the most popular ideas in self-improvement: get 1% better every day. James Clear wrote about it in Atomic Habits. Similar ideas show up as continuous improvement, compounding improvement, kaizen, or the aggregation of marginal gains.

The math is seductive. Improve something by 1%, then improve the improved version by another 1%, and keep compounding those gains. Eventually the result gets ridiculous.

The problem is that humans don't improve like bank accounts. I didn't become 1% faster every time I ran. Sometimes I got slower. Sometimes I got injured. Sometimes I missed days. Sometimes I worked hard for weeks and appeared to make no progress whatsoever.

The 1% idea is useful, but not because human performance follows a smooth exponential curve. The useful part is the accumulation.

A three-mile run barely matters. A single hour learning Python barely matters. One sales call barely matters. One page of writing barely matters. But repetitions change the person doing the repetitions, and that changes everything. The runner on run 449 is not working with the same body as the runner on run 1. The developer making commit 600 is not attacking a problem with the same mental models as the person making commit 1.

That, I think, is where the real compounding happens. Not just in the output. In you.

## The belief preserved itself

A few years ago, if you had asked me whether I could run four miles at an 8:54 pace, I would have laughed. Not because I thought it would be difficult. Because I didn't think I could run. Period.

That distinction matters. For years I had an internal model of myself that said running was no longer something my body did. So I didn't run. And because I didn't run, I never produced any evidence that the model might be wrong. The belief preserved itself.

Then circumstances got painful enough that I violated it. I ran. My body didn't explode. So I ran again. And again. Each run created a small piece of evidence that didn't fit the story I had been telling myself. Eventually the evidence became harder to ignore than the story.

That process has a name in psychology that is much more useful than "believe in yourself." Self-efficacy. Psychologist [Albert Bandura](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.2.191) used the term to describe our belief in our ability to perform a particular behavior or accomplish a particular task. It matters because self-efficacy affects whether we begin, how much effort we put in, and whether we persist when things get difficult. And one of the strongest sources of self-efficacy is something Bandura called mastery experience. Basically: you do the thing successfully. Your brain gets evidence. That evidence changes what you believe you're capable of doing next.

That sounds almost embarrassingly obvious until you compare it with the way we often approach confidence. We tell people to believe in themselves. Think positively. Visualize success. Have the right mindset.

Maybe. But there's another route. Do something. Get a little evidence. Do it again. Get more evidence. You can act your way into a belief that you could never talk yourself into.

## Fake it, correctly

"Fake it till you make it" has a bad reputation because it can sound like pretending to know things you don't know. That's not what I mean. I mean behaving like the person you are trying to become before you fully feel like that person. There's a huge difference. You don't pretend you're an expert. You start doing what an expert-in-training would do.

You speak even though you don't feel like a speaker. You write even though you don't feel like a writer. You lead before the word "leader" feels comfortable. You build software while still thinking, "I'm not really a developer."

Then something strange happens. Reality starts arguing with your self-concept.

That happened to me with software. Two years ago, I couldn't spell Git. That's an exaggeration. Barely. I understood technology. I'd spent most of my career around networks and infrastructure. But software development belonged to software developers, and I wasn't one.

Then I started solving little problems. I wrote a Python script. Then another. Something needed data from an API, so I figured out APIs. I needed somewhere to keep code, so I got more comfortable with Git. Something needed to run automatically, so I learned cron. Then Linux. Databases. Testing. CI. Cloud deployment. Vector databases. Retrieval. Every project exposed another thing I didn't know, so I learned enough to solve the next problem.

The first commit in the body of work I'm doing now was May 2 of this year. Nineteen weeks later I had 165 Python scripts running in production, 56 scheduled jobs, 104 test files, six Git repositories and roughly 600 commits. I built a Next.js website with 19 pages and 145 blog posts, its own CI pipeline and a Cloudflare deployment. I built two prospecting apps. An executive assistant. A video game I built live on stage. A knowledge base with more than 14,000 embedded passages. There are APIs, databases, automation systems, tests and a truly impressive number of things I have broken along the way.

I'm not listing all of that because I think it makes me a great software engineer. It doesn't. I'm listing it because it makes my old sentence, "I'm not a developer," difficult to defend.

At what point does the evidence overrule the identity?

## Identity, in reverse

That question fascinates me, and there is some research behind it. A [2025 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/aphw.70017) looking at habit and identity across health behaviors found a strong relationship between the two. Importantly, the researchers noted that the relationship may not be one-way. Identity can influence behavior, but repeated behavior may also reinforce identity.

That distinction is huge. We tend to think identity comes first. "I am a runner, therefore I run." "I am a writer, therefore I write." "I am a leader, therefore I lead." Sometimes it probably does. But I think the reverse path is just as interesting. I run. Then I run again. I accumulate enough evidence that "I am a runner" starts feeling true. And once the identity changes, behavior changes again.

That may be where friction starts disappearing. Compare these two thoughts:

"I'd like to run more."

"I'm a runner."

The first one contains a negotiation. Should I run today? Do I have time? What's the weather like? Am I tired? Can I do it tomorrow? The second one narrows the decision tree. What does a runner do? Runs.

That doesn't mean identity magically eliminates discomfort. I still have days when I don't want to go. But it changes the default. Running no longer feels like an isolated activity I periodically choose. It is something I do because it fits the person I believe myself to be.

That is enormously powerful. And potentially dangerous if the identity is wrong.

"I'm not technical." "I hate exercise." "I'm bad with money." "I'm terrible at public speaking." "I'm not creative." "I'm not leadership material."

Those statements sound descriptive. But once we adopt them as identity, they can start making decisions for us. They close doors before we ever test them. For decades, "I can't run" did exactly that to me. The way out wasn't better self-talk. It was evidence.

I think that's one reason fake-it-till-you-make-it can work when it's used correctly. You temporarily borrow the behavior before you own the identity. Then you let reality decide whether the identity fits.

## Sunday-night me versus Monday-morning me

This is also where consistency becomes more interesting than discipline. We talk about disciplined people as though they possess some mysterious reservoir of willpower. I'm not convinced. I certainly wasn't motivated for 449 runs. If I had required motivation every time, I would probably have stopped somewhere around 12.

What seems to matter much more is reducing how often you have to make the decision.

Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer's work on implementation intentions is useful here. An implementation intention is basically an if-then plan. Instead of "I'm going to run more," you decide: "After I wake up Monday morning, I put on my running clothes and go." That sounds almost trivial. It isn't. A [meta-analysis of 94 independent tests](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1) found that these kinds of plans had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment. The research suggests they work in part because they make the relevant cue easier to recognize and the desired response more automatic.

That gives me a very different way of thinking about discipline. Maybe discipline is partly the ability to stop making the same decision over and over.

The version of me thinking about my goals on Sunday night is different from the version of me lying in bed Monday morning. Sunday-night me thinks running is important. Monday-morning me can produce a shockingly sophisticated argument for another 45 minutes of sleep. If those two guys have to debate every morning, the wrong one is going to win fairly often.

So remove the debate. Decide earlier. Put the shoes where you can see them. Run at the same time. Use the same route if that helps. Make starting cheap.

That idea transfers far beyond exercise. "I need to learn Python" is vague. "Every weekday at 7:00, I spend thirty minutes building something in Python" is executable. "I need to write more" is vague. "I write 500 words before I open email on Tuesday and Thursday" is executable. "I should build my network" is vague. "I contact two people every Friday morning" is executable.

People often treat consistency as a moral quality. Maybe it's better understood as system design. The fewer decisions between intention and action, the fewer places there are for friction to win.

## The old hard things get cheap

Then repetition does something else. It makes the work cognitively cheaper.

The first time I used Git, nearly everything required conscious thought. Commands. Branches. Commits. Merges. Where am I? What did I just break? Why is Git angry? Eventually, much of that becomes background. I don't have to devote the same amount of working memory to basic operations, which means I can spend more attention on the actual problem.

That's one of the quieter forms of compounding in learning. The old hard things become easy enough that you can spend your cognitive budget on harder things.

A new network engineer may have to consciously think about CLI syntax. An experienced engineer barely notices the syntax and thinks about the system. A new speaker is trying to remember the next sentence. An experienced speaker can notice whether the audience is confused. A new manager is thinking about how to run the meeting. An experienced manager has enough spare attention to notice who hasn't spoken and why.

That's what expertise begins to feel like from the inside. Not knowing everything. Having more mental capacity available because fewer fundamentals require conscious attention.

This is why repetition can create nonlinear returns even though people don't literally get 1% better every day. Run number 449 is not run number 1 plus 448 identical repetitions. Each previous repetition changed the system doing the next one. The same thing happens with software. Script 165 was not written by the same beginner who wrote script 1. That person had already encountered problems, debugged failures, learned tools, built mental models and developed pattern recognition. The next problem was being attacked by a more capable system. Me.

## Repetition needs an edge

This is also why merely showing up isn't enough. You can repeat the same easy thing forever without improving very much.

Research on deliberate practice makes that point pretty clearly. A [major meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810) found that deliberate practice explained meaningful differences in performance across domains, but far less than the popular version of the "10,000-hour rule" would suggest. Practice matters. So do the quality of practice, feedback, difficulty, prior ability, environment and other factors.

The useful takeaway is not "put in enough hours and greatness is inevitable." It's that repetition needs an edge. Something slightly beyond what you can already do.

That has happened naturally for me in software because every useful project eventually drags me into something I don't know. Authentication. Databases. CI. Async code. Cloud deployment. Vector search. Testing. Reliability. The uncomfortable part is usually the curriculum.

Running works the same way. At one point three miles was difficult. Then it became normal. Eventually normal stops producing the same adaptation. You go farther. Or faster. Or uphill. You stress the system enough that it has a reason to change. Not recklessly. Enough.

## Come back

And then there's the part of consistency most people get wrong. Missing.

One of the [classic real-world habit studies](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674) followed people who chose a behavior and repeated it in the same context. The time required for the behavior to approach automaticity varied enormously, from 18 days to 254 days among participants whose data fit the researchers' model. There is no magic 21-day habit rule. But the result I like even more is that missing one opportunity did not destroy the process.

That matters because streaks can fool us into thinking consistency means perfection. It doesn't. If consistency means never missing, everybody eventually fails. A more useful definition might be: come back.

Miss a run. Come back.

Take a week off. Come back.

Get hurt. Recover. Come back.

Stop writing. Open the document again.

Abandon the project. Decide whether it still matters. If it does, come back.

That is probably why I'm still running despite injuries. It isn't because I maintained some beautiful uninterrupted streak. I didn't. It's because stopping temporarily never became the same thing as quitting. There's a big difference.

I think careers work that way too. People get knocked sideways. Layoffs. Bad managers. Projects that fail. Companies that disappear. Skills that become obsolete. Life events that consume all available attention. The person who never gets interrupted does not exist. The person who knows how to restart has a much more useful skill.

## The loop crossed domains

Looking back, getting laid off in 2023 is an almost absurd example. At the time it felt like my life had been disrupted. It had. But that disruption pushed me toward running again. Running made me feel better. Feeling better changed how I thought. Thinking more clearly made problems feel more solvable. Feeling capable made me take more action. Those actions produced new evidence. The evidence changed what I believed about myself. That affected the next set of actions.

The compounding effect wasn't happening in one domain. It was crossing domains. Exercise affected cognition and mood. That affected work. Work created mastery experiences. Those affected confidence. Confidence increased willingness to attempt harder things. Harder things produced new skills. New skills expanded the range of problems I could solve.

That is a much more interesting version of compounding than 1.01 raised to the 365th power.

And there is evidence behind at least part of that loop. A [large 2024 review in BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847) found that several forms of exercise, including walking and jogging, reduced depressive symptoms across randomized trials. Exercise is not a universal substitute for treatment, but the relationship between physical activity and mental health is substantial.

What matters to me is that I didn't know the research when I started. I knew I felt better after running. That was enough. Then feeling better made other things possible.

## Start with evidence, not mindset

The more I think about all of this, the less convinced I am that "mindset" is the right place to start. Mindset matters. But telling someone to change their mindset can become almost useless advice. What if they can't? What if they genuinely don't believe they can do the thing?

I didn't believe I could run. I didn't believe I was a software developer. Trying to force those beliefs probably wouldn't have done much.

Action gave me another route. Do something small enough that your current identity cannot prevent you from attempting it. Then let the outcome become evidence. If the outcome contradicts your old model, pay attention. Repeat. Eventually enough evidence accumulates that the model has to update.

You don't have to begin with "I am a runner." You can begin with "I'm going to run today." Do that enough times and one day the language changes without you forcing it. "I run." Then: "I'm a runner."

That last transition matters, because identity reduces friction. The question stops being whether you should behave that way. The behavior becomes congruent with who you are.

I think that's what people are really chasing when they talk about consistency. Not endless acts of heroic discipline. A point where the desired behavior becomes the normal behavior. Where going for the run requires less internal debate than skipping it. Where writing feels stranger not to do. Where solving technical problems is simply part of how you operate. Where the identity starts carrying some of the load.

But you don't declare that identity into existence. You earn it with evidence.

## Receipts

I started writing this after run 446. Before I finished it, there was 447. Then 448. Today I went out again. Run 449 was 4.05 miles with 221 feet of climbing. My average pace was 8:54. In my first full year back, my average pace was 13:11.

I know comparing one run to a full-year average isn't an apples-to-apples performance comparison. That's not why I care about the number. I care because the guy who averaged 13:11 would have considered an 8:54 pace ridiculous. The guy who thought he couldn't run at all would have considered the entire conversation ridiculous.

Nothing dramatic happened between those versions of me. There were just more days. More miles. More decisions.

The same thing happened in software. One Python script didn't change my identity. Neither did ten. Eventually there were 165 scripts, 56 scheduled jobs, six repos and roughly 600 commits. Those numbers aren't magic either. They're receipts. Evidence that the work happened. Evidence that I'm not the same person who started.

So if there's a formula here, I think it's less inspirational than I expected. Pick something that matters enough that you're willing to keep returning to it. Make the first action small enough that you can do it. Decide in advance when and where it happens. Reduce the opportunities to negotiate with yourself. Keep some evidence.

Miles. Pages. Commits. Calls. Applications. Workouts. Whatever represents the behavior you care about.

When the work becomes easy, make it a little harder. When you miss, come back.

And be careful about the identities you use to describe yourself. Especially the negative ones. You may be treating an old data set like a permanent truth. If you want to become something different, you don't necessarily need to believe the new story yet. Start collecting evidence for it. One piece. Then another. Then another.

At some point you may notice that the argument inside your head has changed. You aren't trying to convince yourself that you can do the thing anymore. You know you can. You've done it too many times to claim otherwise.

That may be what "fake it till you make it" gets right. You behave like the person before you completely believe you are the person. Then you keep going long enough for reality to catch up.

Or maybe it's the other way around. Maybe reality changes first, and eventually your identity catches up with the evidence.

I'm at 449 decisions now. Eventually, if my body cooperates, there will be 500. I doubt run 500 will feel particularly profound while I'm doing it. Most of them don't.

That's probably the point. The decisions that change your life rarely look life-changing when you make them. They look like putting on your shoes. Opening the laptop. Writing the page. Making the call. Submitting the application. Having the conversation. Trying something you're not yet convinced you can do.

And then doing it again.

Eventually you stop saying you're trying to become that person. You look at the evidence and realize you already did.

/Andy
