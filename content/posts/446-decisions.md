On the morning of December 8, 2023, I was in bed with the covers pulled over my head, and I knew that if I stayed there I would never get up.

I knew because I had been there before. Decades earlier, after my first big career loss, I became clinically depressed to the point that my skin hurt. My body weighed a thousand pounds. My world contracted until fighting for my life did not occur to me as an option, because bed was easier than not bed, and I needed to escape how I felt.

Now I had a wife, a mortgage, and kids who depended on my ability to earn a living. My career was in shambles again. And that distant, familiar weight had come back, along with the same instinct: hide. If I got out of bed, reality would be waiting. If I went back to sleep, it wouldn't. Sleep is an escape, and using it that way is a slippery slope toward more deadly ones.

So I lay there under the covers. And I know this sounds crazy, and I still don't know whose voice it was, but I heard something in my head say: *get up. Get the fuck up. Now.*

I got up. I put on my sneakers. I went outside. I put one foot in front of the other.

By three that afternoon I was running around our neighborhood in Southampton, past our kids' grade school. My lungs burned. My legs screamed. My back pleaded with me to stop. And somewhere behind me, in that drill-instructor register, I heard David Goggins: *keep going. If you give up now you die. If you want to live, keep running. Fight for your family. Don't stop.*

So I kept running.

3.35 miles. 11:04 a mile. 232 feet of hill. The watch logged it as "Friday Afternoon Run," which is a very calm name for what it was.

That was run number one.

A few hours ago I finished number 446.

## The curve is a mess

Since that Friday: 1,508 miles. 259 hours. And 446 separate mornings where I decided to start, which is the number I keep coming back to, because I know exactly what the first decision cost.

There is a popular idea that you should get 1% better every day, and the math is seductive because it compounds into something enormous. But people are not compound interest accounts, and my data proves it. Some days I got slower. Some weeks I hurt. Some runs sucked. Sometimes I was injured, sometimes the roads were ice, and sometimes I didn't run at all. Nothing about it looks like a smooth curve.

Here is what it looks like instead. In 2024, my first full year back, I ran 364 miles at an average pace of 13:11 per mile. In 2025 I ran 627 miles, and my average pace was 9:13. Almost four minutes a mile faster. This year I'm at 449 miles through mid-September.

That did not come from being measurably better each morning than the morning before. It came from repetitions piling up while I wasn't watching. The curve is messy. The direction isn't.

## I had the equation backward

I have always assumed it works like this: you believe something is possible, the belief makes you act, the action produces the result. Thought, then action, then outcome.

And for decades that model kept me on the couch. I believed running was over for me, so I didn't run, and because I didn't run, nothing ever showed up to challenge the belief. The behavior protected the assumption.

Then I ran anyway, not because I believed, but because a voice told me to get up. And the running started changing the evidence.

I did not wake up one morning, convince myself I was a runner, and then behave accordingly. I ran. Then I ran again. At some point I had enough runs behind me that saying "I'm a runner" stopped sounding ridiculous. The behavior supplied the evidence for the identity, not the other way around.

Thought, action, evidence, identity. Then eventually just identity and action.

The research on habits points the same direction. Identity shapes what we do, but repeated behavior also builds the identity attached to it. That doesn't prove one causes the other. It does mean you don't always have to believe first. Sometimes you act before you're convinced and let the evidence change your mind.

## Sunday-night Andy versus 6 a.m. Andy

People look at consistency from the outside and assume motivation. I can rule that out. There have been hundreds of mornings I did not want to run.

Cold mornings. Dark mornings. Ice on every road in the county. Brutal heat. Legs that were done before I started.

If this ran on motivation it would have ended in the first winter. What it runs on is a decision I made somewhere other than the moment of action.

Two psychologists, Gollwitzer and Sheeran, studied what they called implementation intentions: deciding in advance what you will do when a specific situation arrives. Not "I should run more," but "when I wake up Monday, I put on the shoes and go." Across 94 separate studies the effect on follow-through was medium to large, d = .65 for anyone who wants the number ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)). The mechanism is simple. The decision has been moved out of the moment when it would have to be defended.

Because here is who is in the room at 6 a.m. Sunday-night Andy wants to get healthier. 6 a.m. Andy wants 45 more minutes of sleep. If they hold a debate every morning, 6 a.m. Andy wins a disturbing number of elections.

A system ends the debate. I learned that under the covers.

## The same thing, with a keyboard

Two years ago I could not spell Git. I'm exaggerating, but barely. Software was something software developers did, and I wasn't one.

Then one Python script, which broke, which meant learning why. Then an API. Then Git, then Linux, then a cron job, each one because the last thing didn't work without it. The countable version of this started on May 2 of this year, when I began keeping everything in version control on a server in my house.

Nineteen weeks later: 165 Python scripts running in production. 56 scheduled jobs. 104 test files. Around 600 commits across eight repositories. A website with 19 pages and its own deployment pipeline. Two prospecting applications. A knowledge base holding 14,176 embedded passages of our podcast. A video game I built on stage at a conference, in front of people.

Things break. I fix them. They break again, and the second time I understand why faster.

I am not claiming 600 commits made me a computer scientist. One of those projects lasted a single day before I abandoned it. Another has been "in progress" since August. But the pattern is identical to the running, and it is the thing I most wanted to write down: I could not get better at Git while avoiding Git. I could not learn debugging without bugs. At some point thought has to become behavior, and the behavior has to happen enough times that the old identity is harder to defend than the new one.

"I'm trying to learn Python" became "I'm building something" became "I build software." Nobody handed me a certificate. The evidence just accumulated until the first sentence sounded wrong.

## The days you don't want to

There is one part the numbers don't hold.

When it is 72 degrees and I'm rested and nothing is on the calendar, a run tells me nothing about myself. When I'm tired and it's freezing and I don't want to go, that decision carries information. Not because suffering is a virtue. It isn't, and there are mornings when rest is the right call. But there are also mornings when "I don't feel like it" is the entire obstacle, and those mornings are not a test of fitness.

Anyone can keep an agreement with themselves when it's convenient. The identity is built when convenience is gone. That may be why those are the runs I remember.

I wasn't practicing running. I was practicing keeping my word.

## 446 votes

James Clear has a line about behavior being evidence for identity. I'd put it a little differently now.

Every repetition is a vote. Not for an outcome. For a person.

Run number one didn't transform me. It got me out of bed, which on that day was the whole job. Neither did 50, or 100. But I have cast 446 votes now, and that is a lot of evidence to argue with. The scripts are the same. One would have changed nothing about how I saw myself. A few hundred forced a question I couldn't dodge: at what point do you stop saying you're trying to learn something, and admit you're someone who does it?

If I could go back to the man under the covers, I wouldn't tell him to believe in himself. That wouldn't have worked. I'd tell him what the voice told him. Get up. Put on the sneakers. Go run badly. Ten minutes. Collect one piece of evidence, then another, and don't worry yet about whether you're a runner.

You don't have to feel ready. You don't need the identity first. You don't need to know Git, or understand the tool, or be confident this will work. You need a direction and a next action small enough to survive, and then you need to keep coming back to it.

The individual decisions look like nothing.

Until there are 446 of them.

/Andy
