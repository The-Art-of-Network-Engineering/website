How the hell do you become a network engineer in 2026?

## The Advice That Got Me Hired

When I started studying for my CCNA around 2010, the advice was pretty straightforward. Get your CCNA. Build a home lab. Practice until you know what you're doing. Find some way to demonstrate that you can communicate.

I followed that advice. I built a lab with old Cisco equipment, started blogging about what I was learning, and eventually made YouTube videos.

That lab wasn't just configs on a screen. I made my own Ethernet cables, straight-through, crossover, rollover, and when they didn't work, I bought a cable tester and figured out why. [One T1 connection taught me something no simulator ever could](https://www.permitipandyandy.com/blog/2016/04/14/youll-never-learn-this-in-gns): it has its own pinout, nothing like the cables I'd already learned.

I [installed a card in my router just to get more ports](https://www.permitipandyandy.com/blog/2015/10/16/card-install-more-ports-yeah). I swapped memory in my 2800s. I built a rack and busted my knuckles on rack nuts more times than I want to admit. I learned the slow stuff nobody puts in a study guide: power management, remote access, cable management, keeping electrical noise out of a run. I even taught myself enough Linux to [configure my own terminal server](https://www.permitipandyandy.com/blog/2015/10/16/%EF%BB%BFconfiguring-a-cyclades-term-server), once I'd learned the hard way which cable it needed in the first place.

When I interviewed for networking jobs, people would tell me I didn't have experience.

Well, hold on. I had routers. I had switches. I'd configured routing protocols. I'd broken things and spent hours figuring out why they weren't working. Sure, my equipment was end-of-life, but I knew how to use it. That lab gave me the confidence to walk into an interview and defend what I knew.

Eventually, someone gave me a shot. So when people ask me how to get into networking today, I want to give them that same advice. Except I'm not sure it's enough anymore. Dakota had some thoughts about that.

## Is the CCNP the New CCNA?

Dakota believes the CCNA is still an excellent certification. But he also thinks we've reached a point where having one doesn't necessarily distinguish you from other candidates. His argument is that we've spent years telling aspiring network engineers to get their CCNA, and they listened. Now hiring managers are looking at piles of resumes from people who followed that advice.

Dakota even suggested that the CCNP might be becoming the new CCNA. That got my attention. When I was coming up, the conventional wisdom was to get your CCNA, land a job, build several years of experience, and THEN pursue your CCNP. Getting a CCNP without production experience was almost frowned upon.

So I pushed Dakota on it. He's a hiring manager. Would he hire someone with a CCNP but absolutely no experience? His answer was no.

Interesting. The certification alone wasn't enough.

But change the scenario slightly. What if that same candidate had a CCNP, an extensive home lab, and a YouTube channel documenting everything they'd learned? Now Dakota was interested. Very interested. Because that candidate wasn't just telling him they understood networking. They were showing him.

## Three Routers I Gave Away

This conversation reminded me of someone I worked with years ago. He was struggling technically, and I thought a home lab might help him get comfortable with routing and switching. I had three Cisco 2620XM routers sitting in my rack, my first routers, the ones I'd spent countless hours learning on. So I pulled them out, drove about an hour, and gave them to him. Free.

He put them in his trunk. He never powered them on.

I still think about that sometimes. Not because everyone needs physical equipment. Dakota and I both love physical labs, but we recognize that money and space can be legitimate obstacles, and Packet Tracer and EVE-NG are useful tools. There's just something about plugging in a cable, troubleshooting a physical connection, and figuring out why your equipment isn't behaving that teaches lessons a simulator might not.

But those three routers weren't really the point. I'd given someone an opportunity to practice. He didn't take it.

Contrast that with a story Dakota told me about one of his own interviews.

## He Hadn't Even Left the Parking Lot

Dakota was interviewing for his first job in IT. Toward the end of the interview, he asked a question he recommends to people today: was there anything he could do before the next interview to become a stronger candidate?

The interviewer mentioned that the organization used Cisco Meraki. At the time, Cisco offered free Meraki equipment through certain webinars. Dakota hadn't even left the parking lot before he was signing up.

He eventually got a firewall, switch, and access point, then integrated all three into his home network. He learned the equipment, configured protocols, built redundancy, and studied the topology. When he returned for his next interview, he explained everything he'd done.

The interview was incredibly short. Why? Because he'd already started doing the work they wanted to hire him for.

I love that story. Not because everyone needs to chase free equipment or spend every waking hour studying, but because Dakota asked what the employer needed, listened to the answer, and did something about it. He turned an interview into a demonstration. From a hiring manager's perspective, that's pretty compelling.

## What Are We Looking For?

At one point, I asked Dakota what makes someone stand out when he's hiring. He talked about communication skills, teamwork, and the willingness to keep learning. His organization has about 25 people, so bringing someone onto the team isn't simply a matter of checking technical boxes. They need to work well with everyone else.

A candidate who can explain their home lab, teach something on YouTube, or discuss a mistake they've made gives him information a certification can't. Can they communicate? Can they learn? Can they work through problems?

And something else came up repeatedly. Hunger.

My wife played college basketball on a full scholarship. She has a saying I've always loved: "You can't teach hustle." Some players dive onto the hardwood for a loose ball. Others don't. Dakota and I kept coming back to that same idea.

But there's another side to this conversation that I think matters just as much. If we're looking for people who want to learn, what are we doing to help them?

## Where Do We Find These People?

I've got three home labs sitting in my basement that I'd love to give away. I've given away equipment before, and the problem isn't finding people who want free stuff. Trust me, announce a giveaway on YouTube and you'll find plenty. The problem is finding someone who could genuinely benefit from it: someone who wants to learn but can't afford equipment, someone trying to change careers, someone who just needs an opportunity.

I asked Dakota how he'd approach that. His answer was mentorship. That sent our conversation in a direction I hadn't expected.

Dakota pointed out that not everyone needs a home lab right now. Someone brand new to IT might benefit more from starting on a help desk, learning foundational skills, and working toward networking. Someone else might already have the technical knowledge but need help interviewing. You can't necessarily solve those two problems with the same advice. You need to understand the person.

Dakota has been building something around that idea.

## Can You Charge for Helping People?

Dakota created the IT Career Accelerator to connect people seeking IT careers with mentors who've worked in the industry, including hiring managers. And he charges for it.

I'll admit, that brought up some feelings for me. I've spent years creating free content and answering questions from people trying to get into networking. The Art of Network Engineering has always been about helping people. So when I hear about paid career programs, part of me gets uncomfortable. We've all seen the expensive boot camps promising to turn someone into an engineer practically overnight.

But Dakota described something different. His program has paid coaching, a free community tier, and scholarships for people who can't afford access. He also pays the coaches for their time.

That last part matters, because something I've been wrestling with is how to keep helping people when there are only so many hours in a day. Every hour I spend answering messages or working on content is an hour I'm not spending with my family. I love doing this, but time isn't unlimited.

Dakota has been experimenting with ways to address that problem. He even built an asynchronous video coaching system so students can send questions and receive personalized video responses from their coaches. He told me he's tracked the outcomes of free and paid coaching, and that people who paid were more likely to follow through and report progress. He didn't have the exact numbers available during our conversation, so I won't pretend we established some universal rule about paid advice.

But his experience raised an interesting question: does putting something into your own development change how seriously you take it? I've certainly experienced that. Putting money and years of effort into Cisco NetAcad kept me in it until I finished. Meanwhile, I've started and abandoned more free Python courses than I care to admit.

Money isn't the only way to demonstrate commitment. But commitment has to show up somewhere.

## The People Already in the Industry Have a Part to Play

Toward the end of our conversation, I found myself thinking about how I got here. I was a cable guy. I spent years climbing poles, installing equipment, and wondering what happened beyond the cable modem. I wanted to understand the CMTS. Then the switching. Then the routing. I wanted to climb the OSI stack.

But I didn't know many network engineers. Eventually, I attended a career fair where someone encouraged me to connect with networking professionals online. So I did. And people helped me. They answered questions. They shared advice. They gave me opportunities to learn.

Dakota challenged experienced engineers to find someone interested in networking and help them along. Maybe that's through a college. Maybe it's LinkedIn. Maybe it's a local user group. Maybe it's someone working a job completely unrelated to IT who doesn't realize network engineering is even an option. I recently spent part of an airport rideshare talking with my driver about getting into technology. I have no idea whether he'll pursue it, but I remember being the guy who didn't know where to start, and I remember what happened when someone pointed me in the right direction.

Dakota's right that aspiring engineers need to put in the work. The CCNA is still worth pursuing. Labs still matter. Communication still matters.

But there's something both sides of the hiring table can do. Candidates can stop waiting for permission to start learning. And those of us already working in networking can make it a little easier for them to find their way.

Someone gave us a shot. Now it's our turn to recognize who's ready for one.
